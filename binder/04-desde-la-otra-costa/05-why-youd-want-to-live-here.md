# Commentary

So, to be clear, the commentary and textual analysis on this piece will not be... the same vibe. The textual analysis will, likely, be on balance positive towards Mr. Gibbard, setting aside the cheap shots one could take regarding his rhyme scheme. Honestly I have too much respect for him as a lyricist.

And not just a lyricist: A lyricist whose work taps so deeply into the poetics of English that his lyrics *resist* translation so strongly. I don't know what his writing process is like, but the result feels so approachable, so "at ease" with the reality of everyday life at the semantic level; and at the level of rhythm and diction, Gibbard's lyrics so often have this internal sense of "chatter"; of quick syncopated patter. 

Both of these points--the sense of rhythm and the sense of everyday life and relatability--are achieved in no small way through the use of low-syllable words, and longer words that can follow, or be forced to follow, the same syllabic pattern. In fact, the longer, more multisyllabic, words, end up being "cute" in their contrast to the more simple language surrounding it.

On the point of translation: the fact that Gibbard uses short words is one of the reasons why it feel *so* natively English, and why it resists translation son strongly: these words are, more often than not, older Anglo-Saxon roots. Germanic. They're farther away from the Romance, so there are fewer loan words, fewer calque-able options. And what's more, the options that *are* available in the Romance are highly likely to be much more multisyllabic, making the translation of the lyrics *as lyrics*, and even more, as lyrics that *fit the time available in the music*, not even mentioning Gibbard's reliable, simplistic poppy rhyme schemes -- often virtually impossible.

One excellent instance of Gibbard matching the syllabic pattern to the rhythmic one is from `We Will Become Silhouettes`, from The Postal Service' *Give Up* album:

Gibbard builds the story through short couplets of monosyllabic words, followed by a line with some clever hook: `I'm not coming out until this is all over`, like a petulant child; then, the second time, two longer lines that repeat that petulance (ok, ok, if I'm being harsh, 'petulant child' might be a generic criticism of the aspects of Gibbard's personality that we can perceive through his lyrics) give rise to the overall argument of the song and the denouement of the first verse: `...I'm screaming at the top of my lungs / Pretending the echoes belong to someone...`

He then repeats the pattern in the chorus: short, low-syllable intro, followed by a higher syllable count line with longer words that, by contrast with their shorter counterparts, become the obvious emphasis at a rhythmic level, further emphasizing their semantic value.

And -- of course, the genius of Gibbard in general and The Postal Service specifically -- is the way that they managed to build a beat and lyrics that work together and mirror each other.

---

Gibbard employs a similar approach in `Why You'd Want To Live Here`, with an additional layer due to the length and complexity of the song. The verses have, generally, a structure like:

| L1 | 1-3 syllables | 8 total | rhyme A |
| L2 | 1-2 syllables | 8 total | rhyme A |
| L3 | 1-2 syllables | 8 total | rhyme B |
| L4 | 1-4 syllables | 8-10 total | rhyme B |

After the first two verses, we have a single, standalone line, consisting of 10 monosyllabic words, which over the course of the song replaces `I'm in Los Angeles today...` as the initial invocation line at the beginning of a verse, setting up a pretty clear pairing between the two: "I'm in Los Angeles today / And I can't see why you'd want to live here".

Regardless of what other comments I may have about Mr. Gibbard here, for the record: The mens rea, the malice aforethought and careful planning that went into this brutal hit piece should stand unassailed. 

Unfortunately, the piece assails itself, which we will get to; but let no one dismiss Mr. Gibbard as some "pop emo" lyricist. He may be a pop emo lyricist; and his work stands as testimony as to why that is, in fact, art.

---

The second layer, the second *trope* in the rhyme structure of this song comes when we get to the bridge. The same pattern we saw in `We Will Become Silhouettes`, and that you can see in *waves hands vaguely at Sub Pop jewel cases* pretty much most of his work, is invoked here; we're contrasting short-syllable story-building with witty, self-deprecating, emotionally vulnerable longer-syllable and more complex, expressions. 

This song has a chorus that's deployed strategically, to emphasize what comes after it. The first time we see it, introduced by our one-liner, it is followed by the bridge, which is the actual emotional and semantic peak of the song--the part of the song that definitively puts a bullet in Ben Gibbard's Los Angeles.

The musical accompaniment of the chorus, as we've seen with Gibbard's compositions, mirrors the function of the semantics. It slows down, from the driving, consistent rhythm behind the "standard" verses, to a slow, deliberate accompaniment, making *absolutely sure you're listening and understand exactly what he's saying*.

And, once he's got our attention, he builds on that attention to execute the kill shot.

Here's the structure of the two bridge verses:

| L1 | 1-3 syllables | 13 total | rhyme A |
| L2 | 1-5 syllables | 14 total | rhyme A |
| L3 | 1-2 syllables | 12 total | rhyme B |
| L4 | 1-2 syllables | 14 total | rhyme B |

Each one of these lines has a solid 5-6 more syllables than your standard line in the song. This first bridge verse is what Gibbard's been building the entire song, rhythmically and structurally, around. 

And then, just like we saw in the chorus of *`Silhouettes`*, Gibbard falls back to the structure-within-a-structure. Poetically speaking, he just got done shouting; now he's the dangerous, quiet kind of angry: Now he's in cold control, and he says the quiet part out loud:

| L1 | 1-2 syllables | 7 total | rhyme A |
| L2 | 1-2 syllables | 8 total | rhyme B |
| L3 | 1-3 syllables | 13 total | rhyme B |
| L4 | 1-2 syllables | 8 total | rhyme C |

Gibbard. King of pop lyrics, capable of putting a brutal, hollow-you-out-and-leave-you-with-a-1000-yard-stare emotional charge into simple A/A-B/B rhyme schemes built of 2-syllable words, *breaks rhythm*?
