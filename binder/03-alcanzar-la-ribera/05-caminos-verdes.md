# Notes:  
In such a short song, it sucks not to be able to nail the rhyme scheme. It's a mantra, and the rhyme is crucial. You can see this in some mantras, but also in folk sayings. There's one that I can't fully remember right now, in Spanish, which is along the lines of `No temas al rio que ... porque el rio que corre no trae maldad`. It was reported to me by a native of Sanlucar de Barrameda, Spain. Her explanation, in context, was that someone who talks a lot is inoffensive, "like water off a duck's back", "easy come, easy go"; that their words are not meant as, well, *poison*: because the original meaning of the saying, as per the Cervantes Institute (https://cvc.cervantes.es/lengua/refranero/Ficha.aspx?Par=58151&Lng=0) was probably intended to pass along wisdom about which streams one should, or should not, drink from.

Terrible example, clearly. Here's another terrible example: `El que a la feria viene / repite.` OK bro.

Context there is apparently it's a very common saying regarding newcomers to the Feria de Abril, a spectacular festival welcoming spring and celebrating traditional dance, dress, and food in Sevilla, Spain, every year. It means, "Whoever comes to the *feria* / Comes back." And it *marginally* has a rhyme scheme in Spanish -- the final `e` vowel -- but generally sayings have much more of a rhythm in Spanish.

Point being: there may be some way to fuck with the syntax of the two rhyming lines, or rework the second line in particular, in order to rhyme `border` with an expression of saving oneself in Venezuela, but I can't think of it... yet.
