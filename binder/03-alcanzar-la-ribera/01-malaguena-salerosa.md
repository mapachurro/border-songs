# Commentary:

Why is this here?

This is not *just* a love song. This is a corollary to a genre that I should look up the history and proper academic research on (`TODO`), but: the `poor boy/empty as a pocket/empty as a pocket with nothing to lose/ ... She's got diamonds on the soles of her shoes`. That genre.

Veáse también: "Me enamoro de ella", Juan Luis Guerra. De lino y de franela. Following *directly* in line with that track, if not explicitly stated as such, it is anyway, "Baile de los pobres", Calle 13. Specifically in line with *this* song, Malagueña Salerosa, is the 1971 Juan Gabriel classic, "No tengo dinero", also covered by Juanes on **Origen**; more on that album [later](04-desde-la-otra-costa/dancing-in-the-dark). 

Point being: This is a song about borders. This is a song from the Mexican point of view, *anhelando*, missing with desire, desiring with lack, `like the deserts miss the rain`, in one of the most contradictory lines ever composed in the genre (the desert *does not, in fact, miss the rain*) the Spanish.

The singer's point of view is imputably male, and the object of desire is grammatically a woman from Málaga, Spain, and she is, I mean this literally, `saucy`. I guess it's actually `salty`, but Christ, that's even worse.

We have an opportunity here to delve into an entire point-counterpoint debate about whether or not we should even engage in a ~~Marxist~~ reading based on economic forces and social class, or whether we should paint it as a "song of its time and period", ~~as if that were ever a valid counterargument to Marxist analysis~~ so let me cut to the chase, by pointing to verses four and five:

```
...Yo te concedo razón  
Si por pobre me desprecias

Yo no te ofrezco riquezas  
Te ofrezco mi corazón  
Yo te ofrezco mi corazón 
A cambio de mi pobreza...
```

This is, quite clearly, a song about una malagueña muy spicy, who also happens to be some degree *wealthier* than the singer. So it's not a question of *if* an economic analysis is merited, it's *a question of degree*.

---

And dimension, for that matter. Arguably the broadest and most intricate treatment of this theme, already portented above, is "Diamonds on the Soles of her Shoes", by Paul Simon. It's a work that is solidly within a world of at least *poetics*, although it presents as, arguably, a work of magical realism. Within the lines already quoted above, we establish the subject (...say it with me now, *the subject is imputably and always implicitly male*, even when the grammar doesn't demand it!) is `a poor boy`, and this appears to be literal, with the poetic descriptors, `empty as a pocket with nothing to lose`.

There are other things that appear to happen literally: `She says 'Honey, take me dancing', but they ended up out sleeping in a doorway / By the bodegas and the lights on upper Broadway`.

Where Simon tends to lose touch with literality is somewhere around `...He makes the sign of a teaspoon / She makes the sign of a wave`. What the fuck even is that? You may ask yourself. Who the hell makes "the sign of a teaspoon", I don't even know how I would, in gesture or symbol, indicate a teaspoon?? 

This is where we're entering the realm of poetics; and with the insistence, very clearly, repeatedly, and *hey, it says it on the label of the song*, that she has diamonds on the soles of her shoes, we are forced to arrive at the only possible conclusion:

"Not all treasure is silver and gold, mate." [^1]

She is so full of love, that it forms a wave; she shines so brightly that ~~she sparkles like a diamond~~ `...you are my diamond / when I'm with you I / shine...`[^2], `from the top of (her) head down to the / bottom of (her) / cowboy shoes`[^3]. And the boy is poor, *impoverished*. Maybe at the level of money; kind of sounds like the level of money, if they're sleeping in a doorway (or maybe they were just hella drunk). 

Which brings us to the reason for which I asser this being one of the broadest and most intricate examples of the genre: we have, in this song, a *hero's journey* embedded inside the genre. The hero (I don't need to say it again, do I?) is a man who is impoverished; the implication is not only that he doesn't have much love for others, it's that he doesn't have much love for himself; and it takes the love of this (grammatically canonically) woman to ~~sigh~~ show him that he is not only worth loving, but that he can love, and he can be as full of love as she is -- he, too, can have diamonds on the soles of his shoes.

---

Perhaps this Malaga Woman is full of the riches of love; that's a reading.

[^1] ...Pirates of the Caribbean. That's a Pirates of the Caribbean reference.

[^2] *You Are My Diamond*, Tijs Verwest, Dennis Waakop Reijers, Kianna Alarid; released by Tiesto ft. Kianna, 2012.

[^3] *Buid Me A Woman*. Original author?? `TODO`; as covered by The Doors, on Absolutely Live.
