Intralinear Subtext

Jimi Hendrix once said that "All Along the Watchtower" felt like a song that he had started writing, but had never finished (TODO: citation). Bob Dylan, the original author of that song, for his part said that song was Jimi's, now (TODO: citation)--words echoed perhaps more awkwardly and fumbling, but no less sincerly, by Trent Reznor regarding Johnny Cash's cover of "Hurt", half a century later(TODO: citation).

So allow me to follow in the vein of my elders and betters: This song always felt, to me, like a Shakira song. It's a Shakira song that just happened to be composed in the wrong language.

Due to accidents of birth and cultural heritage, I was ignorant of Fleetwood Mac until after I had wholesale consumed Shakira's body of work; perhaps there's some explanation there. Perhaps it had to do with encountering this song in its natural habitat: small-town American dive bars at ~1:30AM, while severely under the influence, during a time in my life when I would listen to "Ciega, Sordomuda" on repeat because it spoke to some sense of loss and forgiveness that I couldn't otherwise put a name to or identify.

The point is, it's all there: loss, nature, love; change over time; the will to persevere in the face of it all, bundled in up in the wrapper of a ballad.

It's a Shakira song.

Translator's notes

This song, like several others in this collection (Fast car, Leaving Las Vegas, El Paso, Mexicali Blues...), have spent years getting stuck in my head. And when something comes into my head and is not immediately assigned a location, it gets picked up by the translation machine, which proceeds to grind on it until a higher-priority (aka more recent) project arrives.

I'm looking forward to actually putting it down in writing; maybe that will free up some unfinished side project storage space in the translation machine's spare rooms.

For some reason, maybe the meter or the grandiosity of the song, this line has always been miedo he tenido a cambiar, in my head, instead of he temido or he tenido miedo.

built my life

This is one of the lines that has caused the translation machine to grind its gears, reverse, and attempt to translate properly over and over and over again. edificar is the word that gets used by default. I'm not sure it's correct.

bolder

Yes, más valiente is literal and correct, but dar valor is better. And it rhymes with mayor.

older

As previously stated, mayor would be the correct word to use for children. But when I am getting older, am I becoming mah mayó, or am I becoming más vieja? In which case, shouldn't I envejecer? Too bad I can't amayorar or something.

take my love, take it down

Now, we see the violence foreshadowing inherent in the system! The song begins with her / I / the imputably, but not inherently, female subject "taking (her) love and taking it down". Now, we're at the end of the song, and this initial invocation has become a refrain; it has transformed, as well, from the present indicative first person to the present imperative second person (non-numbered, in English; we can impute "she" is speaking to a singular entity, but we cannot confirm. She may be speaking to us all, or to a specific group. Say... Her fellow bandmates. Oof, fucking oof); this is a command.

Does this change the intended meaning? Should a translation maintain the symmetry of the (very vague, it should be repeated) verbs used here, or should it specify something more?

will bring it down

Once again, we will be forced to Find Out about how we fucked around when we translated the first verse. Also, note: we've now moved from speaking to the second person(s) about the second person(s) to speaking (imputably) to the second person(s) about a third entity, which is, I mean, technically the "subject", that is, "topic", of the song: the implacable forces of time and nature.
